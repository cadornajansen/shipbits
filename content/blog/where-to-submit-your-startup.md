The useful question is not “How many directories can I submit to?” It is “Where can someone with this problem understand and try my product?” A large submission spreadsheet can feel productive while keeping you away from customer conversations.

Start with a small shortlist. Pick one broad discovery channel, one community close to your intended users, and one relevant directory. Work through those deliberately before adding more. This is a planning suggestion, not a formula for traffic: a listing can receive little attention, and attention does not necessarily become usage.

## Choose channels by audience and intent

Write down a specific user and a task. “Software teams” is broad. “A small engineering team that needs to share a temporary test environment” gives you something to evaluate. Look at a channel's recent submissions and discussions. Are people asking questions that your product can answer? Can they actually use what you are launching?

Here are three different kinds of channel to consider:

- **Product discovery communities.** Product Hunt is one option for a launch conversation with other people exploring new products. Its official instructions explain personal-account requirements and what a product post should include. Read those requirements before scheduling your launch. [Product Hunt: how to post a product](https://help.producthunt.com/en/articles/479557-how-to-post-a-product).
- **Hands-on builder communities.** Show HN is for work that people can try, not just a promotional landing page or a signup list. If you have a working developer tool or an interesting technical project, first read the community's guidelines and check whether your account can post. [Show HN guidelines](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html).
- **Relevant directories.** Browse a directory as a potential user would. Does it have understandable product pages, suitable categories, and working outbound links? For products from Filipino builders, [ShipBits' directory](/products) is one place to assess fit. A paid listing is distribution space, not a promise of customers or search rankings.

Rules and access requirements change. Use each service's current instructions rather than an old launch template. Avoid treating communities as places to paste the same advertisement repeatedly.

## Build one accurate submission kit

Keep the reusable facts in one document so that every listing describes the same product:

- Product name and preferred website URL.
- A short tagline that explains its main purpose.
- A concise description of who it is for and what they can do.
- A longer description with a practical example and important limitations.
- A clean logo, a share image, and current screenshots where requested.
- Pricing or access requirements, including whether an account is required.
- A contact route you can monitor after launch.

Write your baseline copy in plain language. For example, “Compare two API responses and highlight changed fields” tells a visitor more than “Transform your workflow with a next-generation platform.” Use features you can demonstrate. If an AI assistant helps draft copy, compare every claim with the actual product before submitting it.

Reuse the facts, but adapt the introduction. A directory needs a quick summary; a community discussion may need the story behind a technical decision. Adapting to the context is different from inventing benefits for each audience.

## Check the destination before sharing it

Open the exact URL in a private browser window and on a phone. Can a visitor understand the product without being signed in as you? Does the main action work? If the product is paid, is the price visible before the visitor commits?

Then check the preview that travels with the link. Use a descriptive page title, a useful meta description, and an Open Graph image that stays legible at a small size. Our [SEO / Launch Checker](/resources/seo-checker) can flag missing basic signals; it cannot tell you whether a person will want the product.

If a channel has image-size requirements, export a version for that channel rather than stretching a tiny logo into a large banner. Keep your original image files so you can update them consistently later.

## Keep a small submission log

A simple table is enough:

| Channel | Why it fits | Date submitted | Public listing URL | Useful response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Your chosen directory | Relevant category | Add the actual date | Add after approval | Questions, trials, or feedback |

Record the real outcome, including no response. Do not invent visits or attribute a sale to a listing just because they happened on the same day. If you already use analytics, consistent campaign parameters can help distinguish referral links. Keep personal data out of those parameters.

Review the results after people have had a chance to respond. One specific question from a suitable user may be more actionable than a large, unexplained visit count. If every visitor asks what the product does, improve the explanation before submitting it to more places.

## Plan the follow-through

Reserve time to answer comments and fix broken links. A launch post with an attentive builder behind it is easier to learn from than an abandoned announcement.

Ask people to try the product or offer feedback, and follow each community's promotion rules. Product Hunt's own launch guidance discourages directly asking for upvotes. That rule is separate from ShipBits' paid-support feature; do not transfer behavior from one platform to another. [Product Hunt launch guide](https://www.producthunt.com/launch).

Before your next submission, work through the [software product launch checklist](/blog/software-product-launch-checklist). The aim is a repeatable, honest launch process—not the largest possible list of logos on your spreadsheet.
